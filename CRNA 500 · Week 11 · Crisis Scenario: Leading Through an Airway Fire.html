CRNA 500 · Week 11

Leading Through an Airway Fire

Scenario

The scene

You are the CRNA providing general anesthesia for a 47-year-old patient undergoing microlaryngoscopy with a CO2 laser to remove a laryngeal papilloma. The patient is intubated with a laser-resistant endotracheal tube. Even with precautions, this is one of the highest fire-risk procedures in anesthesia: an ignition source is being used directly at the airway, inches from the tube delivering oxygen and anesthetic gas. The surgeon is an ENT attending, working through the laryngoscope with a resident assisting. A circulating nurse and scrub tech are also in the room. This scenario unfolds differently from a surface drape fire — when the fire involves the tube itself, the response has to move even faster, and some of the usual steps happen in a different order.

Decision 1: before the laser is activated

The surgeon is about to begin lasing near the tube. As the CRNA, what do you do?

The surgeon activates the laser. A few seconds in, there's a sudden flash and a soft popping sound from the airway. Bright orange flame is visible flickering at the tube, with a thin stream of smoke rising from the surgical field. The surgeon pulls back immediately and says, "It's on fire!"

This is different from a surface fire. The burning material is the tube itself, currently sitting in the patient's airway and still connected to the breathing circuit.

Decision 2: the first action

What do you do in the first few seconds?

The tube is out. A small amount of residual flame and glowing embers are visible at the tip of the removed tube and faintly within the proximal airway.

Decision 3: extinguishing what remains

What's your next move?

The fire is out. The patient is unintubated, apneic, and needs to be oxygenated. You have a bag-mask in hand.

Decision 4: re-oxygenating safely

What do you ventilate with first?

The patient is being safely mask-ventilated with air. You now need to reestablish a definitive airway and find out how much damage the fire caused.

Decision 5: directing the room

How do you organize the next steps?

The difficult airway cart has arrived. Before placing a new tube, you have a brief window to look at what the fire actually did.

Decision 6: assessing the damage

What do you prioritize before reintubating?

The airway shows mild soot staining and localized swelling near the glottis, but no full-thickness injury. The patient is successfully reintubated with the difficult airway team's help. The case is aborted. The patient is stable and will be closely monitored for airway swelling. The room is quiet, and several team members are visibly rattled.

Decision 7: after the airway is secure

What do you do next?

Reflection

An airway fire is not just a smaller, more urgent version of a surface fire — it follows a distinct sequence, because the burning material is inside the airway itself. The core response is: stop the gas flow and remove the tube in the same motion, extinguish any residual embers with saline, re-ventilate cautiously with air rather than high oxygen until the fire risk has passed, reintubate with backup equipment ready rather than assuming a routine airway, and directly assess the airway for injury before committing to a plan.

Layered on top of that technical sequence is the same leadership pattern that shows up across every high-stakes crisis: naming risk before it becomes a crisis, moving decisively without freezing, delegating specific tasks to specific people, resisting the instinct that feels protective but is actually wrong for this situation, and closing the loop afterward with an honest debrief and clear disclosure rather than letting the moment pass in silence.

You haven't been in an OR as an anesthesia provider yet, and that's exactly the point of working through this now: this is one of the highest-stakes scenarios in the specialty, and it's far better to have thought through the sequence once, calmly, than to encounter it for the first time under real pressure.
