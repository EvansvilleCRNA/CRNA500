CRNA 500 · Week 11

Leading Through an OR Fire

Scenario

The scene

You are the CRNA providing anesthesia for a 61-year-old patient undergoing an elective tracheostomy under monitored anesthesia care, with supplemental oxygen delivered by nasal cannula and local anesthesia at the surgical site. This is a classically higher-risk setup for fire: the procedure is on the head and neck, an open oxygen source sits close to the surgical field, and the surgeon will be using monopolar electrocautery to control bleeding throughout the case. The room is fully staffed: a circulating nurse, a scrub tech, a surgical resident, and the attending surgeon. Throughout this scenario, you are the one leading the response.

Decision 1: before the first incision

The team completes the surgical time-out. As the CRNA, what do you do?

Twenty minutes into the case, the surgeon activates the monopolar cautery to control a small bleeder near the incision. A spark ignites a pocket of oxygen-enriched air trapped beneath the surgical drapes. A small flame appears at the surgical field. The scrub tech gasps and steps back. The surgeon's hands freeze mid-air.

Decision 2: the first three seconds

What is your very first action?

You've cut the oxygen source. The flame is small but still burning on the drape near the incision.

Decision 3: removing the fuel

What's your next move?

The tech has pulled the burning drape away from the patient. A small flame is still active on the drape material, now on the floor. The circulating nurse is holding both a basin of saline and a CO2 fire extinguisher, looking to you for direction.

Decision 4: choosing the right tool

Which do you direct her to use, and where?

The fire is out. The room is quiet except for the monitors, beeping steadily. The patient's vitals are stable. Everyone is looking at you.

Decision 5: reassessing the patient

What's your next priority?

Your assessment shows no visible airway injury, and only minor superficial singeing of the drape near the incision, well away from the patient's skin. The surgeon is asking whether you're comfortable continuing.

Decision 6: continue or abort

How do you handle this decision?

The rest of the case finishes without further incident. The patient is stable in recovery. The OR team is still visibly shaken. For several of them, this is the first real fire they've experienced.

Decision 7: before everyone scatters

What do you do before the room turns over for the next case?

Reflection

This scenario followed the real structure anesthesia providers are trained to use when an OR fire occurs: stop the oxidizer, remove the fuel, extinguish with the correct method, reassess the patient, and only then decide whether to continue.

Layered on top of that technical sequence is a leadership pattern that shows up in nearly every high-performing crisis response: naming risk before it becomes a crisis, moving decisively without freezing, delegating by name, matching your tool to the situation instead of grabbing whatever's closest, and closing the loop afterward with a debrief rather than letting the moment pass in silence.

You haven't been in an OR as an anesthesia provider yet, and that's exactly the point of working through this now: most of this thinking is easier to do once, calmly, in a scenario like this than for the first time under real pressure. Use these patterns as the backbone of your one-page written reflection this week.
